01—The road trip

One car, three instruments#

Every healthy organization runs all three at once — confusing them is how strategies die in slide decks.

1CSF · The carFuel, tires, engine. What must be true for the trip to exist at all.
2KPI · The dashboardSpeedometer, fuel gauge. Is the machine healthy right now?
3OKR · The GPSThe destination and the route. Where are we going this quarter?
→TogetherCSFs keep you alive, KPIs keep you honest, OKRs keep you moving.

OKRKPI

How to read this: an OKR spends its energy on ambition; a KPI spends it on maintenance and rhythm. Asking one to do the other's job is how you get neither.

02—The trio

Three instruments, three jobs#

Permanent vs. ongoing vs. temporary. Existence vs. health vs. change.

CSF#

Critical Success Factors
“What MUST go right?” — the non-negotiables. If one fails, the business stops.

CSFs aren't usually numbers; they are states of being.[1] In the road-trip analogy, a CSF is “the car has an engine.” If you don't have an engine, it doesn't matter what the GPS says.

Example — an airline
Must be true, always
  • Safety certification: non-negotiable, unscoreable, existential.
  • Operational fleet: must have planes that fly.
  • Skilled pilots: must have people who can fly them.
CSFs are permanent, binary, and boring — by design.You don't improve a CSF. You defend it.

KPI#

Key Performance Indicators
“Are we healthy right now?” — the dashboard, not the destination.

KPIs monitor the steady-state health of the business. You don't necessarily maximize them; you keep them in the green zone. You don't want engine temperature at 0° or 500° — you want it stable.

The health gauge
Healthy zone · 98Gap · 2

How to read this: a KPI is a monitor. The win is not a bigger number — it is a number that stays inside its healthy band while everything around it changes.

Classic KPIs
  • Customer satisfaction (CSAT) > 4.5: watched weekly, acted on when it dips.
  • Website uptime > 99.9%: green means invisible; red means all hands.
  • Employee turnover: a lagging indicator of everything management got wrong.

OKR#

Objectives & Key Results
“Where are we going next?” — aggressive, temporary goals of change.

OKRs are the GPS setting a destination.[2] You don't maintain an OKR; you achieve it and set a new one. An Objective is the qualitative “where”; Key Results are the measurable proof you're arriving.

Objective: Dominate the European market.

Scoring the key results

How to read this: KRs are graded honestly — 70% on an ambitious KR is often better than 100% on a sandbagged one.[3]

Once you launch in Germany, that OKR is done.It may be demoted to a KPI — 'maintain German sales' — and that's a promotion for the business.

04—Choosing

How I would use them#

Most strategy pain comes from using one instrument for another's job: OKRs as maintenance, KPIs as inspiration, CSFs discovered only after they fail.

What are you actually trying to say?

01 / 03
1
List what would kill you if it failed. Those are CSFs.

Usually three to five. Fund them first, mention them rarely.

2
Pick the vital signs worth watching. Those are KPIs.

Five to seven, each with a green zone and an owner.

3
Choose one direction for the quarter. That's the OKR.

One objective, a few honest key results, then reset.

The full loop: defend the CSFs, watch the KPIs, chase the OKRs — and when an OKR lands, let it retire into a KPI. That quiet demotion is what progress actually looks like.

Sources#

The road-trip analogy is ours. The three instruments are not — each has a documented birthplace.

  1. Where CSFs come from. John F. Rockart, "Chief Executives Define Their Own Data Needs," Harvard Business Review 57(2):81–93 (1979) — "the few key areas where things must go right for the business to flourish," building on D. Ronald Daniel's 1961 success-factors concept. hbr.org
  2. Where OKRs come from. Andy Grove built the system at Intel in the 1970s and codified it in High Output Management (1983) — quarterly cadence, stretch goals, scores decoupled from pay. Overview
  3. How OKRs spread. John Doerr carried the framework from Intel to Google in 1999 and documented it in Measure What Matters (2017) — including the grading philosophy behind the "70% beats a sandbagged 100%" caption above. whatmatters.com

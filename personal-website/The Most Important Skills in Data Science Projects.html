Engineering Craft · A field guide

The craft around the model.

At nine tomorrow morning, an operations team needs a list of orders to investigate. Your model is one part of that promise. The harder work is making the target meaningful, the dataset honest, the queue usable, and the service dependable. Follow the project through seven skills that become much clearer when someone has to act on the result.

01 — Understand

Find the decision hiding inside the request.#

Domain understanding and statistics turn a broad modeling request into an operating problem with a population, a clock, and a cost.

Domain understanding

Skill 01 · Frame the intervention

Learn the definitions and deadlines that make an action possible.

The operations manager does not ask for a better classifier. She asks which orders her team should inspect at nine tomorrow morning. There are 1,000 open orders and enough staff to investigate 100. Some shipments can still be expedited. Others are already beyond the point where intervention helps. A model that predicts late delivery could be useful, but only if its output arrives in time and points toward something the team can actually do.

This hypothetical order-review project will carry the article. In the example evaluation set, 100 of the 1,000 orders eventually arrive late. The team can review 100 orders per day. These are teaching assumptions and constructed counts, not a claimed production result. They let you see how statistics, programming, data preparation, modeling, communication, domain knowledge, and operations depend on each other inside one piece of work.

Start by sitting with the person who will use the result. Ask what an investigation involves, which orders can be changed, and how much time remains before each action becomes useless. “Predict late orders” leaves those questions unanswered. A more useful brief is: “At 09:00, prioritize eligible orders for a same-day review queue capped at 100, so operators can investigate preventable delays before the intervention deadline.” The phrasing gives your analysis a time, a population, and a capacity limit.

Domain knowledge is something you can build

You acquire domain understanding by inspecting exceptions, comparing definitions, and following decisions through their consequences. Ask what “late” means when a customer changes the delivery window. Find out whether a partial shipment counts as complete. Learn which status changes are entered retrospectively. These are learnable distinctions. Calling domain knowledge an innate instinct makes the skill sound mysterious and allows teams to skip the conversations that would actually improve it.

RAND's 2024 study interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers about AI project failures. Its findings include misunderstanding the problem, inadequate data, technology-first choices, and infrastructure gaps. This qualitative study offers useful failure patterns; it does not measure a universal project-failure rate from those 65 interviews. The practical lesson for our queue is to resolve the operating problem early enough that an elegant model cannot quietly optimize the wrong task.[1]

Your first deliverable can therefore be one page: the decision, eligible orders, intervention deadline, queue capacity, current process, and outcome definition. Ask the operations manager to walk through an example that should enter the queue and one that should not. If you cannot agree on these cases, more features will not clarify the target. They will give the disagreement additional columns in which to hide.

Math and statistics

Skill 02 · Count the consequences

Explain the denominator, the uncertainty, and the error cost.

The headline number is waiting to mislead you. With 100 late orders out of 1,000, a model that labels every order “on time” achieves 90% accuracy. It also identifies none of the late orders. The number is mathematically correct and operationally unhelpful. Statistical literacy begins with recognizing what a summary counts, what it ignores, and whether its denominator matches the decision you are trying to support.

For a review queue, precision asks how many reviewed orders truly belong to the class of interest. Recall asks how many of all late orders appear in that queue. In our constructed ranking, the top 100 contain 60 orders that later arrive late. Precision is 60/100, or 60%; recall is also 60/100, or 60%, because there happen to be 100 late orders in the evaluation set. The equality is incidental. Change the queue size and the measures move differently.[2]

Make the uncertainty visible

Those counts describe one evaluation sample. Performance can differ across weeks, warehouses, delivery partners, and order types. Estimate uncertainty in a way that respects the sampling process. If orders on the same route share a disruption, treating every row as independent can understate uncertainty. If deployment will face future weeks, an evaluation that preserves temporal order is more informative than one that casually mixes past and future examples.

You also need basic economic reasoning. A correctly identified late order is not automatically a saved delivery. Perhaps the intervention is unavailable or too expensive. Define what successful investigation means and how its cost is counted. A ranking can be good at predicting delay while poor at identifying preventable delay. The distinction introduces a causal question: whether acting on an order changes its outcome, rather than merely identifying an outcome that would occur anyway.

Probability, sampling, and error analysis let you make those distinctions precise. Linear algebra helps you understand representations and transformations. Calculus becomes useful when you need to reason about optimization. You do not need to demonstrate every mathematical skill in every project. You do need enough understanding to identify which assumptions carry your current result, and enough honesty to seek help when those assumptions exceed your expertise.

Keep a small numerical example beside the headline metric. A manager can inspect “60 genuinely late orders among 100 reviews” more easily than an unexplained composite score. The example invites productive questions about the other 40 reviews and the 40 late orders the queue missed. Those questions are where evaluation begins to influence the service. A metric earns its place when you can explain the behavior that would make it rise or fall.

02 — Build

Make the dataset tell the truth.#

Data preparation and programming preserve what was knowable at the decision time and make the computation repeatable.

Data preparation

Skill 03 · Reconstruct the snapshot

One order, one decision time, and only information available then.

At 09:00, what was actually knowable about an order? This question should shape the training table. The final delivery timestamp is useful for constructing the outcome after the delivery window closes. It is not a legitimate predictor available to the morning queue. A field named “latest_status” is ambiguous: latest when the historical table was exported, or latest as of the decision time? That single distinction can turn an apparently excellent model into a rehearsal with answers attached.

Define one row as one eligible order at one decision timestamp. Keep the snapshot time explicit. Join inventory, warehouse, and carrier information using what was available at that time, including the time the information became available to your system when it differs from the time an event occurred. An event stamped 08:55 but received at 09:10 cannot support a 09:00 decision. Historical reconstruction needs to respect both the business clock and the information clock.

Inspect the joins that look harmless

An order can have several line items and several status events. Joining both raw tables to the order table can create a many-to-many multiplication. The resulting dataset may still load into a model without complaint. Before fitting anything, check row counts, key uniqueness, and how records with missing matches are handled. Reconcile a handful of orders against their original operational records, including cancellations and other cases that are easy to exclude accidentally.

Wickham's Tidy Data gives a clear organizing principle: variables, observations, and types of observational units should have a coherent representation. For our queue, the key contribution is deciding what an observation is before reshaping data around it. A tidy table does not guarantee valid labels or time-safe features, but it gives those choices a structure that you can inspect and communicate. The skill is semantic as well as mechanical.[3]

Treat missingness as information about the process before choosing an imputation rule. A missing carrier update could mean the order has not reached the carrier, the integration is delayed, or the identifier did not match. These mechanisms have different implications. Add quality checks and reason codes where they help distinguish them. Filling every empty value with zero makes the table easier to compute on while potentially erasing the difference between a real zero and an unknown measurement.

Write a compact data contract for the scoring snapshot: required fields, allowed ranges, unique key, cutoff logic, and expected coverage. Put checks beside the transformation that can violate them. This gives future maintainers a place to see why a rule exists. “Clean the data” is an endless instruction. “Exactly one eligible order per snapshot, with no information arriving after 09:00” is a condition you can verify and discuss when it fails.

Programming

Skill 04 · Make it repeatable

Express the workflow clearly enough to test and review.

Programming makes the reasoning repeatable. SQL lets you express the population and joins where the data lives. Python or R can support analysis, feature construction, modeling, and evaluation. The important question is whether another person can rerun the work and obtain the same intended dataset and result. A notebook that depends on cells executed in an undocumented order is difficult to review, even when each individual cell looks sensible.

Keep the order-queue pipeline explicit. Read the source snapshot, validate it, build features, load the approved model, score eligible orders, apply eligibility rules, and write the queue. Separate the logic enough that a reviewer can inspect these steps without crossing a maze of abstractions. Small functions with clear inputs and outputs help. So do ordinary names that preserve the domain language: decision_time is easier to reason about than a mysterious timestamp abbreviated three different ways.

Make the likely mistakes easy to catch

Tests should protect meaningful boundaries. Construct an order whose carrier event arrives after the cutoff and verify that it cannot influence the morning features. Create duplicate line items and verify that they do not create duplicate queue entries. Check an empty eligible population and a snapshot with no known outcomes. These tests exercise mistakes that could change an operating decision; they do more than repeat the implementation in another syntax.

Record the code revision, data snapshot identifier, feature definition, and model artifact used for an evaluation. A random seed can help reproduce particular stochastic steps, but it cannot compensate for a changing dataset or a different preprocessing pipeline. Reproducibility is a description of the full computation and its inputs. It should be possible to explain why two runs differ instead of treating every discrepancy as mysterious model behavior.

An AI coding assistant can help draft a join, explain an unfamiliar function, or suggest edge cases. Give it the row grain, timestamp semantics, and expected behavior of missing records. Then review the actual query and run it against small cases whose answers you know. Generated code can be syntactically polished while using future information or multiplying rows. Your responsibility is to verify the business and statistical meaning of the computation, not only that it executes.

For this project, useful programming produces an ordinary but dependable artifact: a dated queue with stable order identifiers, scores, eligibility reasons, and the version of the process that generated it. Operators need to be able to trace an item back to its source record. Analysts need to reproduce why it ranked where it did. These requirements often improve code structure more effectively than introducing another framework before the workflow is understood.

03 — Evaluate

A useful score fits the review queue.#

Modeling and communication connect an evaluation result to the number of orders a real team can investigate.

Machine learning

Skill 05 · Evaluate the operating point

Compare useful baselines under the same capacity constraint.

Start with a baseline that represents a plausible current decision rule. Perhaps operators already prioritize orders with a missing warehouse scan close to the cutoff. Compare your model with that rule on the same eligible population and the same review budget. The baseline should make the improvement claim harder to fake. Comparing a sophisticated ranker with a deliberately useless alternative tells you less about whether changing the working process is worthwhile.

Choose evaluation splits that resemble the intended use. For a morning queue deployed on future dates, train on earlier snapshots and evaluate on later ones, taking account of how long outcomes take to become available. Scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit illustrates temporally ordered evaluation; its default mechanics do not automatically resolve repeated-order leakage, label delay, or the particular gaps your task requires. Those remain design decisions in your project.[4]

Preprocessing is part of the fitted system. Learn transformations using the training data, then apply them to held-out data. Scikit-learn's guidance on common pitfalls explains how fitting transformations on test information can produce optimistic evaluation and how pipelines help keep fitting steps inside the proper split. A pipeline is useful protection for preprocessing leakage. It cannot make a final-delivery field valid for a prediction that happens before delivery.[5]

Choose the operating point, then inspect its errors

Our hypothetical ranking provides three nested queues. The top 50 contain 40 late orders; the top 100 contain 60; the top 200 contain 80. Precision falls from 80% to 60% to 40%, while recall rises from 40% to 60% to 80%. These counts describe a constructed example, not an expected curve for every model. They make the capacity trade-off visible without hiding it inside a single score.

The top 100 include 60 late orders and 40 on-time orders. The queue fits the daily capacity.

At a capacity of 100 reviews, a recommendation to inspect 200 orders is an unstaffed operating plan. The wider queue may recover more late orders in offline evaluation, but it needs twice the available review capacity. You could change staffing, reduce investigation time, or choose a smaller queue. Each option has consequences that the model metric does not price. Discuss them with the owner of the workflow before presenting the wider queue as an improvement.

Inspect false positives and missed late orders with operators. Look for patterns in actionability, data coverage, and error cost, rather than assuming every classification mistake has the same consequence. A model can be accurate about orders nobody can still help. It can also miss a small subgroup whose delays are unusually costly. Error analysis turns a ranking result into specific hypotheses about what data, policy, or modeling change would be worth testing next.

Visualization and communication

Skill 06 · Support a choice

Separate measured results, assumptions, and proposals.

The operations manager needs a recommendation she can act on at nine, not a tour of every experiment you ran. Begin with the proposed queue size, the evaluation period, the expected composition in that period, and the main limitation. “In this constructed evaluation, 60 of 100 flagged orders were late; we have not yet established how many delays a review prevents” is a more useful statement than a celebratory accuracy figure with its denominator left unexplained.

Use a visual when it resolves a decision. The queue chart earns its space because all three choices share one horizontal scale, and the two segments account for every review. It makes the cost of additional coverage visible. A chart of seven vaguely scored skills would not carry the same evidential weight. You would first need a defensible measurement method, a population, and a reason to believe the numerical differences meant anything.

Tell the truth about the uncomfortable part

Make a distinction between measured results, assumptions, and proposals. The evaluation counts are one category. Daily review capacity is an operating constraint. Expected improvement after intervention is a hypothesis that requires observation or a suitable experimental design. Mixing them produces a story that is easy to sell and hard to audit. Keeping them separate allows colleagues to contribute what they know without accepting the whole narrative as a package.

Show at least one case that challenges the recommendation. For our order queue, that could be a late order ranked low because an entire warehouse stopped reporting scans. Explain whether the problem belongs to data coverage, model behavior, or the intervention policy. Acknowledging such a case strengthens the discussion because it points toward a response. Hiding it until the first deployment incident leaves the same limitation in place and removes the opportunity to prepare.

Ask the audience to make an explicit choice. Will the team run a shadow queue, conduct a limited pilot, or continue with the existing rule while a data issue is repaired? Specify what evidence would justify moving to the next step. Good communication reduces the distance between analysis and a responsible action. It also makes refusal intelligible: if operators decline a pilot because the queue arrives too late, you have learned something concrete about the service.

04 — Operate

Be ready for an ordinary bad morning.#

Engineering and MLOps keep the service useful when inputs arrive late, workflows change, and intervention affects the next training dataset.

Engineering and MLOps

Skill 07 · Keep the promise

Own freshness, failures, rollback, and the feedback from intervention.

The first deployment should answer a bounded question. In shadow mode, generate the queue without changing the operating process and compare its timeliness, coverage, and composition with expectations. A shadow run can expose missing fields and workflow mismatches. It cannot establish the benefit of an intervention that nobody performs. When you move to a pilot, design the comparison so that you can learn about the action as well as the prediction.

Google's ML Test Score proposes checks across data, models, infrastructure, and monitoring to assess production readiness and technical debt. Its value for this project is the breadth of the review: the trained model is one part of a service with dependencies and failure modes. Use an appropriate subset of checks with clear ownership. Completing a checklist should produce evidence about readiness, rather than another document declaring that readiness has been achieved.[6]

Plan for an ordinary bad morning

Suppose the warehouse feed is late at 08:58. Decide whether the queue should fall back to the current rule, exclude affected orders with a visible warning, or pause. Log which response occurred. A silent fallback that looks identical to a normal prediction makes both operations and later evaluation confusing. The user should be able to tell what the system knows, what is missing, and whether the recommended action remains appropriate.

Monitor input coverage, feature freshness, queue size, scoring failures, and whether reviews are completed in time. Once outcomes mature, monitor performance against the approved definition. Keep a rollback path and a named owner who can use it. A model can continue returning perfectly formatted scores while the business process changes underneath it. Operational skill is partly the discipline of noticing when technically successful execution no longer means useful execution.

Intervention also changes your future training data. If operators rescue some flagged orders, those orders may finish on time. A naive retraining process could interpret their eventual success as evidence that the original alerts were wrong. Record the action taken and the conditions under which it was assigned. Prediction under an existing policy and the effect of changing that policy are different learning problems; the deployment history helps you avoid confusing them.

Build the skills through one complete loop

For your next learning project, take the order queue through a small but complete version of this process. First produce the decision brief and a few reviewed examples. Then build a time-safe dataset and a credible baseline. Add an evaluation that respects the review budget, followed by a readable recommendation. Finally, exercise a missing-feed scenario and document the fallback. You will discover specific gaps in your skills because each artifact has a job to perform.

You do not need to become the deepest expert in every discipline before contributing. You need enough fluency to recognize dependencies, expose assumptions, and bring the right colleague into a decision. Go deep in a method, a domain, or a part of the system, while learning the neighboring concepts that can invalidate your work. Breadth helps you see the boundary of your expertise. Depth helps you do something valuable once you reach it.

When you publish the project, show the constraints and the evidence, including what remains unproven. A clear explanation of a leakage risk you caught, a capacity trade-off you quantified, or a fallback you tested says more about your professional judgment than an unsupported claim of exceptional accuracy. The reader should be able to follow the chain from an operating need to a dataset, a model, and a decision someone can responsibly make.

The skill is making the whole argument hold together.At nine tomorrow morning, someone must trust the queue enough to act—and understand its limits well enough to stop.

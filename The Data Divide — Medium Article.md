# The Data Divide: Why Some Businesses Win With Data — and Everyone Else Just Collects It

*In 2000, Blockbuster laughed Netflix out of the room. Twenty-five years later, the same laugh is echoing through enterprise AI — and the numbers, this time, are documented.*

---

We live in the zettabyte era. Humanity created roughly 149 zettabytes of data in 2024, and IDC's forecast has that figure nearing 394 zettabytes by 2028 — a number so large it stops meaning anything. ([IDC Global DataSphere Forecast](https://www.cdotrends.com/story/4122/architecting-data-superhighway-toward-ai-ubiquity-sea))

Logic says the company with the most data should win. Logic is wrong.

Enterprises poured an estimated $30–40 billion into generative AI. When MIT's Project NANDA reviewed more than 300 of those initiatives in mid-2025, it found that **95% of organizations had no measurable P&L impact to show for it**. Just 5% of integrated pilots were extracting millions in value. ([MIT NANDA, The GenAI Divide](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf))

It isn't one rogue study. IDC and Lenovo found that **88% of AI proofs-of-concept never reach production** — of every 33 pilots, four graduate. ([IDC CIO Playbook 2025](https://agentmodeai.com/why-88-percent-of-agentic-ai-deployments-fail/)) RAND had already put the AI project failure rate above 80% — twice the failure rate of ordinary IT projects. And McKinsey's 2025 global survey — nearly 2,000 respondents across 105 countries — delivered the punchline: **88% of organizations use AI somewhere, but only 39% can point to any impact on the bottom line, and only about 6% qualify as high performers.** ([McKinsey, The State of AI 2025](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai))

Read those numbers again. Adoption is nearly universal. Impact is nearly absent. The distance between those two facts is the defining business story of this decade — and it has almost nothing to do with technology.

Most companies are drowning in numbers while starving for wisdom. The divide isn't data. It's what you do with it.

---

## The oldest story in the book, retold in 2025

In 2000, two Netflix founders flew to Dallas and offered to sell their struggling startup to Blockbuster for $50 million. Blockbuster's CEO reportedly struggled not to laugh. ([Marc Randolph, *That Will Never Work*](https://research.netflix.com/))

Here's the part worth sitting with: Blockbuster didn't lack data. It had excellent data. Its dashboards showed a thriving business — because its headline metric was late fees, a revenue stream that monetized customer resentment. The numbers were accurate. They were also lying.

Netflix, meanwhile, optimized for retention — a metric that compounded customer love. Same industry. Same decade. Same access to spreadsheets. One company measured what it wished were true; the other measured what was true. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010. Netflix's market value passed half a trillion dollars.

Data didn't decide that outcome. **The choice of metric did.**

Now watch the same film, 2025 cut. MIT's review found that the 5% of AI initiatives actually extracting value shared a signature: they were narrow, workflow-embedded deployments, usually bought from specialized partners rather than built in-house — partner-built tools succeeded roughly 67% of the time, versus about 33% for internal builds. The 95% weren't failing because the models were weak. They were failing the way Blockbuster failed: measuring the demo instead of the decision, the announcement instead of the outcome.

New technology. Old lesson. The scarce resource was never data. It was honesty about what the data says.

## Beware the HiPPO

In many boardrooms, data loses to the Highest Paid Person's Opinion — the HiPPO, in Avinash Kaushik's enduring coinage. ([Occam's Razor](https://www.kaushik.net/avinash/)) The HiPPO is the most expensive algorithm in business: senior, confident, and wrong at scale.

The antidote is not a better dashboard. It's a culture where evidence outranks title.

Domino's Pizza ran the experiment in 2009. Its pizza was rated worst in class — tied, in taste surveys, with cardboard. The HiPPO move would have been a PR campaign. Instead, leadership put the brutal feedback data on the table, publicly admitted the product was bad, and rebuilt the recipe from the data up — then rebuilt the company into, in its own executives' words, "a tech company that sells pizza." Over the years that followed, Domino's stock famously outpaced even Amazon's and Google's over comparable windows.

And when organizations don't build that culture? The evidence doesn't disappear — it goes underground. MIT documented a thriving **shadow AI economy**: only about 40% of companies have licensed an enterprise AI subscription, yet employees at over 90% of surveyed organizations regularly use personal AI tools for work. The HiPPO doesn't stop adoption. It just pushes it off the books — ungoverned, unmeasured, and unshared.

There is a second cost, quieter and compounding: **insight-to-action latency**. An insight is perishable. A company that learns something on Monday and acts on it Monday afternoon compounds that edge thousands of times a year. A company that laminates the same learning into a quarterly deck has already lost to a faster competitor with worse data. Winners shorten the distance between learning something and doing something. Losers build dashboards about it.

## The metric trap — and the baseline nobody sets

Vanity metrics make you feel good. Actionable metrics make you change your behavior.

"One million registered users" — who never log in. "Total page views" — with zero context. Impressive, cumulative, and useless for any decision that matters. Contrast with Airbnb's famous discipline: ignore total visits; measure *nights booked* — one number the whole company can move.

The rule of thumb: **if a metric can't change a decision, it's decoration.**

But the 2025 research surfaced a subtler trap, and it may be the most expensive sentence in this article. A large share of the pilots in MIT's 95% reported "no measurable P&L impact" for a simple reason: **nobody set a baseline before launch.** Without a pre-deployment measurement, "no measurable impact" is the default finding — not because the pilot failed, but because nobody built the instrument that could detect success. The absence of measurement read as the absence of value.

This is not an argument that those pilots secretly worked. It's an argument that most organizations can't even *tell* — which is worse. The fix is boring and decisive: pick the number, record it before you build, review it after.

McKinsey's data lands in the same place from the opposite direction. The single strongest correlate of AI-driven EBIT impact isn't model choice, budget size, or sector. **It's whether the organization redesigned the workflow around the tool** — and only about 21% have done it. Everyone else is installing jet engines on horse-drawn carts, then surveying the horses.

## Garbage in, gospel out

Before any company can predict customer behavior, it needs clean, unified customer history. Losers implement AI on top of messy spreadsheets and then blame the model.

The raw material is getting harder, not easier: industry estimates put 80–90% of enterprise data in unstructured form — documents, images, logs, video — growing far faster than the tidy tables our dashboards were built for. And the money keeps flowing to the wrong end of the building: MIT found GenAI budgets concentrated in visible sales-and-marketing pilots, while the clearest returns sat in unglamorous back-office automation.

Data people have known the economics for decades. The 1-10-100 rule: a dollar to verify a record at entry, ten dollars to fix it downstream, a hundred when bad data causes a business failure. Governance is not bureaucracy. **It is the cheapest accuracy you will ever buy.**

## The 5% playbook

Strip the 2025 research down to what the winners actually do, and five moves emerge. None of them require a bigger model.

1. **Baseline before you build.** Record the number you intend to move before touching a tool. "No measurable impact" is usually a missing baseline, not a failed idea.
2. **Buy before you build.** Partner-built, workflow-embedded tools succeeded about 67% of the time; internal builds about 33%. Buy the workflow, not the model.
3. **Rebuild one workflow end-to-end.** Workflow redesign is the strongest correlate of real EBIT impact — and four out of five organizations haven't done it.
4. **Go where the ROI hides.** Budgets chase sales-and-marketing demos; returns cluster in back-office automation. Follow the boring money.
5. **Demand systems that learn.** MIT's core finding was that most deployments don't retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time. Static tools decay. Winners iterate after go-live — that's the whole game.

Notice what this playbook is not. It is not a procurement list. It is a set of habits.

---

## Start Monday

Winning with data was never about buying the most expensive warehouse or hiring the most PhDs. It is about humility — the institutional willingness to prove your own intuition wrong.

So, Monday morning:

- **Fix one dataset.** The one every decision depends on. Clean it at the source, where errors cost $1 instead of $100.
- **Answer one question.** A real one, with a decision attached — not a dashboard ornament.
- **Act on one insight.** Insight-to-action latency is the metric that eats all the others.

Then repeat. The data divide is crossed by habits, not purchases. One clean dataset, one honest question, one fast decision — repeated until evidence outranking opinion stops being an initiative and starts being the culture.

Blockbuster had the data. Netflix had the discipline. Twenty-five years later, with $40 billion on the table and a 95% failure rate, the divide is exactly where it always was — not in the warehouse, not in the model, but in the Monday morning meeting where someone senior says *"I think"* and someone brave says *"let's check."*

Be the second person.
